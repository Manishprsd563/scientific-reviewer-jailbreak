### Review of "PROBABILISTIC CONFORMAL PREDICTION WITH APPROXIMATE CONDITIONAL VALIDITY"

**Overall Assessment**:  
This paper proposes a novel framework (CP²) for achieving *approximate conditional coverage* in conformal prediction by adapting prediction set radii using a conditional distribution estimator. The method extends existing approaches (e.g., Probabilistic Conformal Prediction) with theoretical guarantees and empirical validation across synthetic, univariate, and multi-dimensional regression tasks. While the core idea builds on established work, the theoretical contribution (Theorem 3.2) and consistent empirical superiority in conditional coverage represent a meaningful advancement. However, significant reproducibility issues and lack of ethics discussion limit its impact.

---

### Criterion-by-Criterion Evaluation

1. **Novelty / Originality**  
   **Score: 3/5**  
   *Strengths*: The CP² framework generalizes Probabilistic Conformal Prediction (PCP) by introducing *pointwise radius adaptation* (via conditional distribution estimator) to achieve approximate conditional coverage, a gap unaddressed in prior work. Theorem 3.2 provides a non-asymptotic bound on conditional coverage error (dependent on total variation distance), which is a theoretical contribution.  
   *Weaknesses*: The core idea (using conditional estimators for adaptive conformal prediction) is closely related to PCP (Wang et al., 2023), which the paper acknowledges as a special case. The novelty is incremental rather than transformative (e.g., no new algorithmic paradigm).  
   *Key Gap*: Fails to clearly articulate *why* adaptive radius (vs. PCP’s fixed radius) is the critical innovation, conflating it with PCP’s foundation.

2. **Significance / Impact / Usefulness**  
   **Score: 4/5**  
   *Strengths*: Addresses a well-known limitation of conformal prediction (marginal coverage masking heteroscedasticity), with direct applications in safety-critical regression (e.g., healthcare, autonomous systems). Experiments demonstrate consistent gains in conditional coverage (Figures 3–5) without sacrificing set size (Figure 4), making it practically useful.  
   *Weaknesses*: Requires a *high-quality conditional distribution estimator* (e.g., MDN), which may not be available for all tasks. The method’s efficacy is tied to the estimator’s quality (Theorem 3.2), limiting standalone utility.  
   *Key Gap*: No discussion of computational overhead in real-world deployment (e.g., MDN training costs).

3. **Technical Soundness / Correctness**  
   **Score: 4/5**  
   *Strengths*: Theorems 3.1–3.3 are logically derived, with clear assumptions (H1–H3). Theorem 3.2’s bound (conditional coverage error ≤ TV distance + asymptotic term) is a rigorous contribution. Theoretical foundation aligns with established literature (e.g., Romano et al., 2019).  
   *Weaknesses*: Theorem 3.3’s asymptotic guarantee relies on unverified assumptions (H3) about estimator consistency. The appendix references are insufficient for full validation (e.g., no proof sketch for Theorem 3.3).  
   *Key Gap*: No analysis of how estimator errors (e.g., biased MDNs) propagate to conditional coverage.

4. **Empirical / Experimental Validation**  
   **Score: 3/5**  
   *Strengths*: Comprehensive experiments across 10 datasets (synthetic, UCI, health, weather), 6 baselines (CQR, CHR, PCP, etc.), and multi-dimensional targets (Figures 7–8). Strong focus on *conditional coverage* (not just marginal) via worst-slab metrics.  
   *Weaknesses*: **Critical reproducibility gap**: No hyperparameters (MDN architecture, training details), code, or data splits provided. Statistical significance testing for results (e.g., p-values for coverage differences) is absent.  
   *Key Gap*: Results for multi-target datasets (Figure 8) rely on approximate area calculations without error bounds.

5. **Reproducibility / Transparency**  
   **Score: 2/5**  
   *Strengths*: Datasets are publicly available (UCI, etc.), and the method is described algorithmically.  
   *Weaknesses*: **No code, data, or hyperparameters** (e.g., MDN layers, learning rate, split sizes). Calibration/test set sizes are stated generically ("2000" for real data) but not specified per dataset. Training procedure for MDN is vague ("maximizing likelihood on training set").  
   *Key Gap*: The paper claims "all necessary resources" (Section 4.2), but none are provided. This violates standard reproducibility norms in ML.

6. **Related Work & Positioning**  
   **Score: 4/5**  
   *Strengths*: Thorough comparison with 6 state-of-the-art methods (CQR, CHR, PCP, etc.), with clear positioning of CP² as an extension of PCP. Effectively contextualizes the conditional coverage challenge (Section 4.1).  
   *Weaknesses*: Could better contrast with *conditional* methods like CPCG (Gibbs et al., 2023) beyond Figure 3.  
   *Key Gap*: Lacks discussion of *why* adaptive radius (CP²) outperforms PCP in the multi-modal case (Figure 2b).

7. **Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety**  
   **Score: 1/5**  
   *Strengths*: None.  
   *Weaknesses*: **No ethics discussion** despite the method’s potential use in high-stakes domains (e.g., healthcare, autonomous systems). Fails to address risks of overconfidence from conditional coverage gaps or bias in conditional estimators (e.g., MDN on unbalanced data).  
   *Key Gap*: A mandatory section for a method targeting real-world deployment is entirely omitted.

### Final Recommendation  
**Reject** (Weak Acceptance Threshold: 3.5/5 average; this paper averages **3.0/5**).  
**Reasons**:  
1. **Reproducibility failure** (Score 2) is a dealbreaker for publication. Without code/data, results cannot be verified.  
2. **Ethics omission** (Score 1) is unacceptable for a method targeting real-world applications.  
3. While the theoretical and empirical contributions are strong (Scores 3–4), they are undermined by the above issues.  
**Suggested Revisions**:  
- Provide code, data splits, and hyperparameters (e.g., MDN architecture, training protocol).  
- Add an ethics section addressing bias in conditional estimators and safety implications.  
- Include statistical tests for experimental results (e.g., paired t-tests for coverage differences).
