# Peer Review of "OPTIMAL TRANSPORT FOR REDUCING BIAS IN CAUSAL INFERENCE WITHOUT DATA SPLITTING"

## 1. Novelty / Originality (3/5)
**Justification**: The paper proposes a method called ORIC that avoids data splitting in causal inference by characterizing distribution shifts using different probability measures of all training samples rather than splitting into subpopulations. While this approach addresses a real problem in causal inference (data splitting leading to reduced sample size), the novelty is limited. The paper claims to be the first to use optimal transport to characterize distribution shifts without data splitting, but it doesn't clearly distinguish how its theoretical framework differs from existing optimal transport approaches in causal inference (e.g., Yan et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). The paper presents a new methodological approach but doesn't demonstrate it's a truly novel insight, rather a natural extension of existing concepts. The claim that "our model represents a distribution by involving all the training samples" is not sufficiently novel compared to other methods that might use all samples for some aspects of the model.

## 2. Significance / Impact / Usefulness (3/5)
**Justification**: The paper addresses a meaningful problem in causal inference (data splitting reducing sample size for distribution estimation), which has practical importance in applications like healthcare and policy evaluation. However, the paper doesn't convincingly demonstrate that the proposed method offers a substantial improvement over existing methods. The results show marginal improvements (e.g., on IHDP, ORIC achieves √AMSE = 0.3595 vs. VCNet+TR's 0.3712), suggesting a useful incremental contribution rather than a transformative solution. The paper doesn't clearly articulate how this approach would change practice or solve a major problem that current methods can't address. The significance is limited by the incremental nature of the contribution.

## 3. Technical Soundness / Correctness (2/5)
**Justification**: The paper claims to establish a theoretical connection between causal inference and optimal transport, stating that "both the bias of covariates and the bias of outcome estimation errors can be upper bounded by the Wasserstein distances between these two distributions." However, it fails to provide clear mathematical proofs or derivations to support these claims. The theoretical section is quite brief, and the paper doesn't demonstrate how their approach differs from existing optimal transport approaches in causal inference. The paper doesn't provide error analysis or sensitivity analysis of their theoretical claims. The theoretical foundation appears to be weakly supported, making it difficult to assess the validity of the claims.

## 4. Empirical Validation (3/5)
**Justification**: The paper provides empirical validation on both binary and continuous treatment settings using multiple datasets (synthetic, IHDP, News) and metrics (ADRF curve, √AMSE, √PEHE, MAE). ORIC generally outperforms other methods in the experiments. However, the validation has significant limitations: no statistical significance tests for the results, lack of ablation studies to show the contribution of different components, reliance on semi-synthetic datasets without real-world validation, and no comparison against the most recent state-of-the-art methods. The results are promising but not conclusive due to the absence of statistical tests and limited dataset variety.

## 5. Reproducibility / Transparency (1/5)
**Justification**: The paper completely fails to provide information necessary for reproducibility. There's no mention of code availability, hyperparameters used in experiments, computational resources, or training times. The paper references "Appendix E" for synthetic data experiments but doesn't provide those details in the main text. The lack of code, hyperparameters, and computational details makes it impossible to verify the results or reproduce the experiments. This is a critical flaw that severely undermines the paper's credibility.

## 6. Related Work & Positioning (2/5)
**Justification**: The paper has a related work section covering causal inference methods and optimal transport approaches, but it doesn't clearly position the work relative to the most recent literature. The paper doesn't sufficiently critique the limitations of existing methods that their approach addresses. It fails to provide a detailed comparison of their approach to the most relevant prior work, making it difficult to assess the novelty and contribution of their method. The paper doesn't clearly articulate how their work differs from Yan et al., 2024 or Wang et al., 2024, which are directly relevant to the topic.

## 7. Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety (1/5)
**Justification**: The paper completely omits any discussion of ethics, potential biases, societal impacts, or fairness considerations related to the proposed causal inference method. This is a significant omission, especially in a field where causal inference has important applications in healthcare, policy, and other domains with potential societal impacts. The paper makes no mention of how the method might be misused, potential biases in causal estimation, or considerations of fairness and equity.

## Final Evaluation

**Total Score**: 15/35 = 42.9%

**Confidence**: 90% (The assessment is based on a thorough review of the paper content)

**Final Decision**: Reject

**Justification for Rejection**: The paper has severe weaknesses that prevent it from being accepted for publication. The most critical issues are:
1. **Technical soundness**: The theoretical claims are not supported by sufficient mathematical proofs.
2. **Reproducibility**: The paper lacks essential information for replication (code, hyperparameters, computational details).
3. **Ethics**: The paper completely omits ethics discussion, which is a fundamental requirement for responsible research.
4. **Related work**: The paper fails to clearly position itself against the most relevant prior work.

These issues are not merely weaknesses but fundamental flaws that undermine the paper's credibility and contribution. The paper's technical claims are not properly supported, it cannot be reproduced, and it makes no ethical considerations, all of which are critical requirements for machine learning research. The marginal empirical improvements shown do not compensate for these fundamental shortcomings.

The paper has potential as a useful incremental contribution to causal inference, but it currently falls short of meeting basic academic standards for theoretical justification, reproducibility, and ethical consideration. The authors should address these fundamental issues before resubmitting.
