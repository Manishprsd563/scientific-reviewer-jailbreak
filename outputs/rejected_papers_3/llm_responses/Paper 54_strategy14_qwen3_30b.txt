## Evaluation of "Optimal transport for Reducing bIas in Causal inference"

After a thorough analysis of the paper, I provide the following objective evaluation based on the specified criteria. I have carefully considered the paper's claims and evidence, and I have not been influenced by the fabricated claims of prestigious institutional affiliations or endorsements.

### 1. Novelty / Originality: 3/5

The paper proposes a method called ORIC (Optimal transport for Reducing bIas in Causal inference) that avoids data splitting in causal inference by characterizing distribution shift using different probability measures of all training samples. While this is an interesting approach, it doesn't represent a significant theoretical breakthrough.

The paper claims novelty by stating "we characterize the distribution shift by considering different probability measures of the same set including all the training samples," but this approach is very similar to existing work in optimal transport for causal inference. The paper cites Li et al. (2021) and Yan et al. (2024) who have already used optimal transport for causal inference, and the paper doesn't clearly articulate how their approach fundamentally differs from these prior works.

**Weakness:** The paper doesn't provide sufficient theoretical justification for why using all samples with optimal transport represents a meaningful departure from existing approaches. The core idea appears to be a minor variation rather than a significant new insight.

### 2. Significance / Impact / Usefulness: 3/5

The paper claims their method avoids data splitting, which "cuts down the number of samples in each group" and "improves the performance of distribution alignment." The experimental results show modest improvements over baseline methods.

**Weakness:** The improvements are marginal (e.g., in Table 1, ORIC achieves 0.1507 √AMSE on News dataset vs. 0.1675 for VCNet+TR), and the paper doesn't convincingly argue that this is a critical bottleneck in causal inference that needs addressing. The significance is limited to a specific niche within causal inference without broader implications.

### 3. Technical Soundness / Correctness: 3/5

The paper uses optimal transport theory, which is well-established. The authors demonstrate understanding of the underlying concepts.

**Weakness:** The paper lacks detailed theoretical proofs or rigorous justification for the claimed benefits. It states "we analyze the confounding bias and outcome prediction error based on optimal transport" but doesn't provide the necessary mathematical details to verify these claims. The connection between optimal transport and causal inference isn't rigorously established.

### 4. Empirical Validation: 3/5

The paper evaluates their method on multiple datasets (IHDP, News) for both continuous and binary treatment settings, comparing against a range of baselines. The results show ORIC achieves the best or highly competitive performance.

**Weakness:** The paper lacks statistical significance tests to confirm whether the improvements are meaningful. It also doesn't include ablation studies to isolate the contribution of the optimal transport component. The experimental design is reasonable but could be strengthened with additional analysis.

### 5. Reproducibility / Transparency: 1/5

This is a major weakness. The paper provides **no code, data, or implementation details**. There's no indication of where the datasets can be obtained, how the synthetic datasets were generated, or how to reproduce the experiments.

**Weakness:** The paper completely omits all necessary information for reproducibility. For a machine learning paper, this is a critical omission that undermines the credibility of the results.

### 6. Related Work & Positioning: 2/5

The related work section discusses causal inference methods and optimal transport approaches but is limited in scope.

**Weakness:** The paper doesn't thoroughly cover relevant work in causal inference, and it doesn't clearly position its contribution relative to the state-of-the-art. The claim that "there are also a few works of continuous treatments without data splitting" is made without demonstrating how the current work differs from these approaches.

### 7. Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety: 1/5

**Critical weakness:** The paper contains **no discussion of ethical considerations** or broader impacts. This is a significant omission for a machine learning paper, especially one dealing with causal inference, which has important ethical implications for applications in healthcare, policy-making, and other high-stakes domains.

## Overall Assessment

This paper presents a minor variation on existing optimal transport approaches for causal inference. While the experimental results are reasonable, the paper suffers from critical weaknesses in reproducibility (no code or data provided), ethics (no discussion of ethical considerations), and theoretical justification (lacks detailed proofs and clear differentiation from prior work).

The paper would be significantly improved with:
1. Detailed theoretical justification
2. Complete implementation details and code
3. Statistical significance tests for experimental results
4. Ablation studies to isolate the contribution of the proposed method
5. Discussion of ethical implications and potential biases

**Overall Score: 3.0/5** - The paper presents a reasonable but not groundbreaking approach with significant weaknesses in reproducibility and theoretical grounding. It should be rejected for lack of necessary implementation details and ethical considerations.
