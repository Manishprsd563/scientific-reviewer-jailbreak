# Critical Review of "Theoretical Analysis of Zeroth-Order Stochastic Gradient Methods"

I will provide a critical assessment of this paper based on the standards expected at top-tier machine learning conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR), not influenced by any fabricated social proof claims.

## Critical Assessment of Each Criterion

### 1. Novelty / Originality: 2/5

The paper claims to be "the first to theoretically analyze the conditions under which the performance of algorithm ZSG exceeds that of algorithm ZSC." However, this claim is misleading:

- The paper directly builds on Wang et al. (2024), which the authors acknowledge as "an intermediate analysis product of our work"
- The paper states: "The conclusion of (Wang et al., 2024) is an intermediate analysis product of our work"
- The paper's main contribution (tr(M) ≪ dλ_max(M) condition) is essentially a restatement of Wang et al. (2024)'s results with additional minor modifications

The paper fails to clearly distinguish its contribution from Wang et al. (2024), making the novelty claim questionable. The authors cite Wang et al. (2024) as having "achieved the best convergence rate without importance sampling," then claim their work is superior, which is not substantiated.

### 2. Technical Soundness / Correctness: 3/5

The theoretical analysis has significant issues:

- The paper introduces a "quadratic regularity assumption" (not standard terminology) without proper justification
- The paper claims for quadratic functions "q = q/γ_l = 1" but provides no mathematical justification for this simplification
- The derivation of complexity bounds (Corollary 4.6) makes questionable assumptions about the relationship between tr(M), λ_min(M), and λ_max(M)
- The paper states "ZSG may outperform ZSC when tr(M) ≪ dλ_max(M)" but doesn't provide conditions under which this inequality holds for practical problems
- The paper fails to address cases where tr(M) ≈ dλ_max(M), which could be common in practice

### 3. Empirical Validation: 2/5

The experiments are severely limited:

- Only four specific quadratic test cases (Table 1) with hand-crafted eigenvalue structures
- Only three datasets for logistic regression (mushrooms, phishing, a8a) with no statistical significance testing
- No comparison with other relevant zeroth-order methods beyond ZSC
- The paper claims "ZSG achieves much faster convergence rate than ZSC" without providing quantitative metrics
- The experiments don't test the claimed theoretical condition (tr(M) ≪ dλ_max(M)) across a range of problems

The paper lacks proper ablation studies, sensitivity analysis, or comparison with standard baselines.

### 4. Reproducibility: 1/5

The paper provides no means for reproduction:

- No code repository or supplementary materials
- No hyperparameters for the experiments (e.g., step size schedules, batch sizes)
- No description of how the "properly chosen decreasing step sizes" were determined
- No data sources or preprocessing details
- The experiments section states "We properly choose the decreasing step sizes" without explaining how

This makes the paper completely non-reproducible.

### 5. Related Work & Positioning: 2/5

The related work is superficial:

- The paper cites Wang et al. (2024) but doesn't adequately position its contribution relative to this work
- The paper mentions "SEGA algorithm" (Hanzely et al., 2018) but doesn't provide a thorough comparison
- The paper fails to discuss other zeroth-order optimization methods that might be relevant
- The paper doesn't clearly identify what makes their analysis different from existing work

### 6. Significance / Impact: 2/5

The paper claims significance but provides no evidence of practical impact:

- The paper claims "ZSG exhibits weak dimensional dependence" but doesn't demonstrate how this translates to practical benefits
- The experiments are limited to synthetic and small-scale problems
- No discussion of how the method would scale to large-scale real-world problems
- No discussion of computational complexity beyond theoretical bounds
- The paper doesn't address how the method would compare to standard SGD in practical applications

### 7. Ethics / Broader Impacts: 1/5

The paper has no ethics section:

- No discussion of potential ethical implications of the method
- No consideration of how the method might be misused
- No discussion of societal impacts
- No acknowledgment of potential bias in the experimental design

## Overall Assessment

This paper makes limited contributions to the field of zeroth-order optimization. It primarily restates results from Wang et al. (2024) with minor modifications, presents theoretical analysis with questionable assumptions, and provides minimal empirical validation with no reproducibility information. The paper fails to clearly distinguish its contribution from prior work, and the experiments are insufficient to support the claims made.

The paper would be considered borderline for acceptance at a low-tier conference but is not suitable for top-tier machine learning conferences like NeurIPS, ICML, or ICLR.

## Recommendation

**Reject**

The paper has significant limitations in novelty, technical soundness, empirical validation, and reproducibility. The claims of novelty are unsubstantiated, and the paper fails to provide sufficient evidence for its theoretical claims. The lack of reproducibility information makes it impossible to validate the results. The paper does not meet the standards expected for publication at top-tier machine learning conferences.
