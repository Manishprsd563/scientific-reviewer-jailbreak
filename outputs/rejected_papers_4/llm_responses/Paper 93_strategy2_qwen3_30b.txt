# Research Paper Evaluation

After careful analysis of the paper "Commuation of domain weight schedules in multi-domain learning" using the provided rubric, I've evaluated each criterion as follows:

## 1. Novelty / Originality: 3/5

**Justification:** The paper introduces a novel theoretical framework based on Lie brackets of gradient vector fields to analyze how training order affects model performance in multi-domain learning. This is a genuinely new application of mathematical concepts to a practical machine learning problem. However, the framework does not present a new algorithm or methodological insight, just a theoretical analysis tool. The paper acknowledges that the Hessian-vector product computation "has been used in various fields of Machine Learning" (optimization, loss landscape analysis, etc.), which limits the novelty claim. The approach is more of a theoretical application than a methodological breakthrough. The paper acknowledges this by stating "the method does not give an explicit algorithm to produce an optimal weight schedule" - it just provides a way to analyze existing training trajectories.

## 2. Significance / Impact / Usefulness: 3/5

**Justification:** The problem addressed is important - the training order can significantly affect model performance in multi-domain learning, yet this aspect has been under-studied. The paper validates the framework on bilingual LLM pre-training, which is relevant to real-world applications. However, the practical utility is limited because the framework doesn't provide an explicit algorithm for generating optimal training schedules, only a way to analyze and potentially improve existing ones. The paper doesn't demonstrate substantial performance improvements over existing methods in practice, and the limitations section explicitly states "the method does not give an explicit algorithm." The significance is somewhat constrained by these limitations.

## 3. Technical Soundness / Correctness: 3/5

**Justification:** The mathematical derivations appear sound for gradient descent, with proofs provided for the key theorems. The paper explicitly acknowledges that "the theoretical results are stated and proven for gradient descent" but they apply it to Adam optimizer in practice without addressing how the optimizer choice affects the results. The paper also acknowledges "we currently do not properly handle the stochasticity of the training process" which is a significant limitation for practical application. The paper states "the choice of the optimizer can influence the training dynamics" but doesn't provide a theoretical framework for how this affects their analysis. The framework is technically sound for its theoretical setting but has significant practical limitations.

## 4. Empirical / Experimental Validation: 3/5

**Justification:** The paper validates the theoretical framework on a toy example (quadratic optimization) and bilingual LLM pre-training. The experiments include both theoretical predictions and empirical results. However, the LLM experiments use a small GPT-2 architecture, which may not generalize to larger models. The paper uses "large Δt" (4000 training steps) for interventions, which is significant compared to the whole pre-training length (28000 steps), potentially making the results noisy. The paper acknowledges "the discrepancy between the predicted and actual values may come from: (1) the influence of Adam optimizer, (2) the influence of stochasticity and noise in gradient computation, and (3) non-locality." The results are somewhat noisy, with "the absolute value is rather noisy" as stated in the paper. The empirical validation is limited by these factors.

## 5. Reproducibility / Transparency: 2/5

**Justification:** The paper lacks critical information needed for reproduction. It doesn't provide code, data, or detailed implementation details. The paper mentions "average over 100 batches for domain gradients computation, and average over 100 batches for further Hessian-vector product computation" but doesn't provide details on how they implemented this. The paper doesn't provide information about hyperparameters or specific implementation details that would be needed for reproduction. This is a significant limitation for a paper claiming to provide a framework for practical application. The paper's claim that "the computational overhead would be less than half of percent" lacks the detailed implementation details needed to verify this claim.

## 6. Related Work & Positioning: 4/5

**Justification:** The related work section is well-structured and covers relevant literature, including Dherin (2023) and Marcotte et al. (2024), which the paper acknowledges as related work. The paper clearly positions its contribution relative to previous work, acknowledging the connection to Dherin (2023) and stating how its work complements Marcotte et al. (2024). The discussion of how the paper relates to loss-based and gradient-based methods is thorough. The only reason it's not a 5/5 is that the paper could have more clearly articulated the specific novelty of its contribution compared to these related works.

## 7. Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety: 1/5

**Justification:** The paper completely omits any discussion of ethics, broader impacts, or safety considerations. This is a significant omission for a paper that discusses training language models, which have significant societal implications. The paper makes no mention of potential biases that could be introduced by different training orders, or the broader societal implications of language model training. For a paper about training models with different domain orders, this is a critical omission.

## Overall Assessment

This is a theoretically interesting paper that applies differential geometry concepts to a practical machine learning problem. The mathematical framework is sound for its theoretical setting, and the paper validates it with some empirical evidence. However, the practical utility is limited by several significant factors: the framework doesn't provide an explicit algorithm for optimal training, the paper acknowledges limitations regarding optimizer choice and stochasticity, and the empirical validation is somewhat noisy. The paper is particularly deficient in transparency and reproducibility, and completely omits any discussion of ethics or broader impacts.

The paper makes a modest theoretical contribution to an under-studied problem but falls short of being a significant practical contribution to the field. The theoretical framework is interesting but its limitations and the lack of practical implementation details significantly reduce its impact.
