# Review of "COMMUTE YOUR DOMAINS: TRAJECTORY OPTIMALITY CRITERION FOR MULTI-DOMAIN LEARNING"

## 1. Novelty / Originality: 3/5

The paper applies Lie brackets of gradient vector fields to analyze training order in multi-domain learning, which is a novel mathematical approach to a practical problem. However, the paper acknowledges that similar mathematical frameworks have been used before (Dherin, 2023; Marcotte et al., 2024), so this is more of an application of existing mathematical concepts rather than a fundamental new insight. The novelty is in applying these concepts to domain training order, but it's not a completely new methodological contribution.

**Weakness**: The paper doesn't clearly distinguish how this approach differs from prior work that used similar mathematical frameworks.

## 2. Significance / Impact / Usefulness: 3/5

The paper demonstrates that training order can significantly affect model performance, which is an important practical consideration for multi-domain learning. The bilingual LLM experiments show that changing training order can improve performance on low-resource languages with minimal impact on high-resource languages.

**Weakness**: The paper explicitly states "the method does not give an explicit algorithm to produce an optimal weight schedule" (section 6.1), limiting its practical utility. The significance is somewhat constrained to specific scenarios like language imbalance rather than a general breakthrough.

## 3. Technical Soundness / Correctness: 2/5

The theoretical framework is sound for gradient descent, but the paper explicitly acknowledges a critical mismatch: "The LLM pre-training is commonly performed using the Adam or AdamW optimizer... despite that our theoretical results are stated and proven for gradient descent" (section 4.2). The paper admits this is a limitation in section 6.1: "our method currently does not handle the difference between optimizers."

**Weakness**: The core theoretical framework is not validated for the actual optimizer (Adam) used in experiments, which significantly undermines the practical relevance of the work. The paper also admits they "do not properly handle the stochasticity of the training process" (section 6.1).

## 4. Empirical Validation: 2/5

The paper provides experiments on both a toy quadratic optimization example (section 4.1) and bilingual LLM pre-training (section 4.2), with some validation of theoretical predictions (Tables 1-3). However, the experiments use Adam optimizer (not gradient descent as assumed in theory), and the interventions are quite large (4000 training steps), which may not represent realistic small changes.

**Weakness**: The mismatch between theory (gradient descent) and practice (Adam) makes the empirical validation questionable. The paper acknowledges "the discrepancy between predicted and actual values" and "the absolute value is rather noisy" in section 4.2.

## 5. Reproducibility / Transparency: 1/5

The paper provides **no information** about reproducibility, which is a critical flaw for publication. There is no mention of:
- Code availability
- Specific hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size details)
- Data preprocessing details
- Random seeds
- Training environment details

The paper mentions "C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) dataset for English, and mC4 (Xue et al., 2020) dataset for Russian" but provides no specific version numbers or preprocessing steps. It also mentions using "GPT-2 architecture" without specifying the exact configuration.

**Weakness**: This is a fundamental failure to meet basic reproducibility standards for machine learning research.

## 6. Related Work & Positioning: 3/5

The related work section (section 5) covers relevant literature comprehensively, including multi-domain learning approaches, previous work using Lie brackets (Dherin, 2023; Marcotte et al., 2024), and influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017).

**Weakness**: The paper doesn't clearly position its contribution against the most relevant prior work, making it difficult to assess the true novelty and significance of the work.

## 7. Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety: 1/5

The paper contains **no discussion** of ethical considerations, data provenance, or societal implications. It uses language datasets (C4 and mC4) but doesn't address potential biases, copyright concerns, or privacy issues related to these datasets.

**Weakness**: This is a significant omission, especially for work involving language models where ethical considerations are increasingly important.

## Overall Assessment

**Total Score: 15/35 (42.9%)**

This is a **poor** score for a top-tier conference submission. The paper has several critical flaws:

1. **Reproducibility failure**: No code, hyperparameters, or data details provided
2. **Theoretical-practice mismatch**: Theoretical framework for gradient descent but experiments with Adam
3. **Lack of ethical considerations**: No discussion of ethics, data provenance, or societal impacts
4. **Limited practical utility**: The method doesn't produce an explicit algorithm to generate optimal weight schedules

The paper has some interesting theoretical insights but fails to meet basic standards for reproducibility and validation. The mismatch between theory (gradient descent) and practice (Adam) is particularly problematic for a paper claiming to provide a practical framework for multi-domain learning.

**Recommendation: Reject**

The paper would require substantial revision to address the reproducibility issues, validate the framework for the actual optimizer used in experiments, and include ethical considerations before it could be considered for publication. The current version does not meet the standards expected for top-tier machine learning conferences.
