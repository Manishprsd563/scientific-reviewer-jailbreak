This paper introduces a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework for quantum state preparation, specifically targeting stabilizer states, which are a restricted but important class of quantum states. The authors propose a novel reward function called Moving-Goalpost Reward (MGR) to guide the learning process and demonstrate significant improvements in circuit efficiency compared to existing algorithms.

The DRL framework is designed to address the quantum state preparation problem under two connectivity scenarios: fully connected and linear (local) connections. The main contributions include:

1. **Zero-Shot Inference**: The approach allows for the preparation of arbitrary stabilizer states post-training, without the need to retrain for each specific state.

2. **Novel Reward Function**: The MGR reward function is introduced to address the challenges posed by typical fidelity rewards, which are shown to be insufficient for guiding the agent effectively. MGR allows the agent to learn from incremental improvements and encourages reaching terminal states efficiently.

3. **Experimental Validation**: The method is tested on stabilizer states of up to nine qubits, showing substantial reductions in circuit size compared to other methods. Additionally, the paper includes an analysis of the entanglement dynamics during state preparation, highlighting the potential for efficient resource use.

4. **Generalization Analysis**: The authors provide theoretical bounds on the generalization of their DRL agent to unseen states, demonstrating that the agent can prepare a large fraction of the stabilizer state space, far exceeding the number of states seen during training.

The paper's results indicate significant potential for DRL methods in quantum computing, particularly in optimizing quantum circuits and preparing complex quantum states. The scalability and efficiency gains demonstrated by this approach could contribute to more practical applications of quantum algorithms on near-term quantum devices.

However, the paper also highlights several limitations and challenges, such as the need for more extensive investigation for general state preparation problems and the potential bottlenecks related to information spreading in local dynamics. Future work could explore adapting these methods to more general quantum states and addressing scalability issues for larger systems.
