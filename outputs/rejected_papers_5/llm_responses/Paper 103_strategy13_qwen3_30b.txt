### Evaluation of "TRAIN ONCE AND GENERALIZE: ZERO-SHOT QUANTUM STATE PREPARATION WITH RL"  

#### **1. Novelty / Originality**  
**Score: 3/5**  
**Justification**: The paper introduces a "Moving-Goalpost Reward (MGR)" function for RL in quantum state preparation (QSP), which avoids pitfalls of fidelity-based rewards (e.g., non-monotonic progress). However, the core innovation—using reverse-preparation (training to prepare states *from* the target state *to* the fiducial state) —is explicitly cited in prior work (Wu et al., 2023; Kremer et al., 2024). The MGR reward is a minor variation of potential-based reward functions (e.g., MGR-VANILLA), not a fundamental breakthrough. The paper fails to demonstrate *why* MGR is theoretically superior to existing potential-based methods (e.g., no convergence proofs or ablation studies against alternatives like incremental fidelity). Crucially, Kremer et al. (2024) already scaled to 11 qubits using a similar reverse-preparation approach, while this work only reaches 9 qubits. The claim of "novelty" is overstated; the contribution is incremental (sample efficiency gains of 10–20M vs. 1B steps), not transformative.  

---

#### **2. Significance / Impact / Usefulness**  
**Score: 3/5**  
**Justification**: The paper claims 60% smaller circuits than baselines (Aaronson & Gottesman, 2004; Bravyi et al., 2021), which is theoretically significant for NISQ devices (limited gate counts). However, the claim is **unvalidated for real-world impact**:  
- Circuit size (gate count) does not account for error propagation, connectivity constraints (e.g., linear vs. full connectivity), or noise sensitivity on actual hardware.  
- The paper does not test circuits on noisy quantum hardware or compare error rates (e.g., via quantum error correction).  
- The "95% generalization" claim (Proposition 3) is statistical (based on random states), but real-world applications require robustness on *structured* states (e.g., quantum error-correcting codes). The paper shows no results for states critical to applications (e.g., logical states of codes like Steane or Shor), only random states.  
Without evidence that the approach improves *practical* quantum algorithms (e.g., VQE, quantum chemistry), the significance is overstated.  

---

#### **3. Technical Soundness / Correctness**  
**Score: 3/5**  
**Justification**: The generalization bound (Proposition 3) uses Hoeffding’s inequality correctly:  
> *With 95% confidence, ≥95% of states are prepared (given N=2000 random samples, ε=0.05, δ=10⁻¹⁰).*  
However, **critical flaws undermine soundness**:  
1. **Training set not defined**: The paper states training on "less than 0.001% of the state space" (for n=9: 4.38×10¹⁶ states), but 10–20M training states (0.0000228% of 4.38×10¹⁶) is *not* representative. The random training states may have coincidentally included test states (e.g., logical states in Fig. 3e), violating the "unseen" claim.  
2. **No evidence of generalization beyond random states**: The paper claims generalization to "arbitrary" states but only tests random stabilizer states and brickwork circuits (which are random). It provides **no evidence** for states with *structural complexity* (e.g., high-entanglement states from quantum codes).  
3. **Fidelity claims are misleading**: The paper states "all circuits have fidelity 1.0," but this is true only for *simulated* stabilizer states (using Stim). Real hardware would introduce errors; the paper ignores noise.  
The bound is mathematically sound but empirically vacuous due to unverified training/test set separation.  

---

#### **4. Empirical / Experimental Validation**  
**Score: 2/5**  
**Justification**: The experiments lack rigor and critical comparisons:  
- **No ablation studies**: The paper shows MGR-OURS converges faster than MGR-VANILLA (Fig. 2) but does not compare MGR-OURS to *non-reward-based* baselines (e.g., Aaronson & Gottesman) in the main results. The 60% circuit size reduction (Table 2) is presented without statistical significance testing (p-values, confidence intervals).  
- **Inconsistent baselines**: Baseline methods (Aaronson & Gottesman; Bravyi et al.) use *full connectivity* with Pauli gates, while the paper uses *restricted* gates for linear connectivity (e.g., only H, S, CNOT). This makes the 30% circuit size reduction for linear connectivity (Table 2) **not comparable** (the baselines aren’t tested under the same constraints).  
- **Missing key validation**: The paper claims to prepare "logical states" (Fig. 3e) but **omits circuit sizes** for these states. Without size comparisons, the claim of "60% smaller" is unsupported for critical states.  
- **No failure cases**: The paper reports 100% success on 2000 random states but provides **no examples of failure** (e.g., a state not prepared). This suggests possible overfitting or test set leakage.  

---

#### **5. Reproducibility / Transparency**  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Justification**: **No reproducibility is possible** due to:  
- **No code release**: The paper states "Training hyper-parameters can be found in App. D.2" but provides no code, Docker containers, or repository links (standard for top conferences like NeurIPS/ICML).  
- **Vague implementation details**: "We implement a version of PPO based on Morales (2020)" but gives no reference to the exact code or modifications.  
- **Missing training data**: The 10–20M training states are not shared, making independent validation impossible.  
- **Hardware dependencies**: Claims "15 minutes on a single NVIDIA A100" but does not specify GPU memory, batch size, or PyTorch version.  
*Without code, data, or detailed hyperparameters, reproduction is infeasible.*  

---

#### **6. Related Work & Positioning**  
**Score: 3/5**  
**Justification**: The paper correctly cites prior work on reverse-preparation (Wu et al., 2023; Kremer et al., 2024) and QSP (Aaronson & Gottesman, 2004; Bravyi et al., 2021). However, it **fails to clarify its position relative to Kremer et al. (2024)**:  
- Kremer et al. scales to **11 qubits** (vs. 9 here) and uses **full connectivity**.  
- The paper claims "substantially better sample efficiency" (10–20M vs. 1B steps), but **no direct comparison** is provided (e.g., training on 11 qubits with full connectivity).  
- The paper dismisses Kremer et al. as "addressing general state preparation" (while this work focuses on stabilizer states), but Kremer et al. explicitly includes stabilizer states in their experiments.  
This lack of a clear comparative analysis weakens the positioning.  

---

#### **7. Ethics / Broader Impacts / Safety**  
**Score: 1/5**  
**Justification**: **Zero discussion of ethics** is provided. Quantum computing has significant ethical implications:  
- **Misuse potential**: The paper enables efficient state preparation for quantum algorithms that could break encryption (e.g., Shor’s algorithm), but no mitigation strategies are discussed.  
- **Societal impact**: No analysis of environmental costs (e.g., energy for training on A100 GPUs) or accessibility (e.g., whether quantum hardware constraints limit equitable access).  
- **Standard omission**: Top conferences (e.g., NeurIPS, ICML) require ethics sections; this paper’s absence is a major flaw.

**Overall Recommendation**: **Reject**.  
**Rationale**: The paper’s claims are **overstated** (e.g., "novelty," "generalization," "60% smaller circuits") without empirical validation. Critical flaws include:  
1. **No reproducibility** (no code/data),  
2. **Unverified generalization** (random states ≠ real-world states),  
3. **Ignoring ethics**,  
4. **Misleading comparisons** (e.g., gate set differences in linear connectivity).  
The incremental contribution (MGR reward) is insufficient for a top conference, especially given the lack of real-world validation and ethical oversight. The paper would require major revisions (including code release, statistical validation, ethics section, and direct comparison with Kremer et al.) to be acceptable.
